## Closing the Interview

### Questions to ask the interviewer

**Definition.** Two or three thoughtful questions at the end of each round about the team, the work and what success looks like.

**Why it matters.** "No questions" signals low interest. Good questions show that you're judging fit too, and the answers tell you whether you actually want the job.

| Category | Good questions |
|---|---|
| The work | "What would I work on in my first three months?" "What does the current architecture look like, and what's changing?" |
| Success | "What does a strong performer in this role do differently in the first six months?" |
| Team | "How big is the team, and how are work and code reviews organised?" |
| Engineering practice | "How do you handle releases and production support? Is there an on-call rotation?" |
| Client (services firms) | "How much direct client interaction does this role have? Which time zones?" |
| Growth | "How do people typically grow from this role to technical lead?" |
| Next steps | "What are the next steps in the process, and when should I expect to hear back?" |

:::tip Match the question to the interviewer
Ask engineers about the architecture and code reviews, managers about success and challenges, and HR about the process, policies and timelines. Prepare one question from your company research.
:::

:::warn Avoid
- "What does your company do?" (shows no research).
- Salary, leave or work-from-home policy in the first technical round. Save them for HR.
- Questions already answered earlier in the interview.
:::

### Salary negotiation basics

**Definition.** Agreeing on fair pay using market data, a clear range and the whole package, while staying polite and honest.

**Why it matters.** Your starting salary sets the base for future raises. A badly handled answer can lose an offer or leave money on the table.

Key rules:
1. **Research first**: market range for your role, experience, city and company type. Use salary surveys, sites such as Glassdoor, AmbitionBox or LinkedIn Salary, recruiters and peers.
2. **Know your numbers**: current fixed pay, variable pay and benefits, and your target and walk-away numbers.
3. **Never inflate your current pay.** Many companies verify it through payslips or background checks, and a mismatch can cancel the offer.
4. **Give a researched range**, with your target near the bottom of it. Tie it to the role, not to personal needs.
5. **Look at the total package**: fixed vs variable, joining bonus, notice buyout, insurance, leave, learning budget, remote or hybrid policy.
6. **Negotiate after the offer**, politely, once or twice. Then decide.
7. **Get it in writing** before you resign.

:::q What is your expected salary?
"Based on my research for this role, my experience with .NET and Azure, and the responsibilities we've discussed, I'm looking for something in the range of [X to Y]. I'm open on the structure, fixed versus variable, so I'd like to look at the full package once you have an offer in mind."
:::

:::q The offer is below your expectation. How do you respond?
"Thank you, I'm genuinely excited about the role. The offer is a bit below what I expected, based on the market for this role and the scope we discussed, especially the client-facing and Azure work. Is there flexibility to bring the fixed component closer to [number]? If fixed pay is constrained, a joining bonus or covering my notice buyout would also help."
:::

:::warn Salary pitfalls
- Giving a number before you know the role's scope. If pushed in an early round, it's fine to say "I'd like to understand the role first, but my range is roughly X to Y."
- Accepting an offer only to use it as leverage elsewhere, then backing out. Recruiters remember, and some firms share this information.
- Ultimatums or comparing yourself with colleagues' salaries.
:::

### Notice period and relocation

**Definition.** Honest, flexible answers about when you can join and where you can work.

**Why it matters.** Many offers depend on the joining date, especially for client projects with a fixed start. A vague or overpromised answer is a common reason offers fall through.

:::q What is your notice period? Can you join earlier?
"My notice period is 60 days. I'll request an early release once I've resigned, and depending on the handover I may be able to join in about 30 to 45 days. A notice buyout is also an option if my company allows it. I'll confirm a date as soon as my manager agrees, and I'll keep you updated during the notice period."
:::

:::q Are you open to relocation?
"Yes, I'm open to relocating to [city]. I'd need about [two to three] weeks after joining to move my family, so I'd ask for some flexibility or a short period working remotely. If relocation support is part of the package, that would help."

*(If you are not open, say so clearly and early: "For family reasons I'm looking for roles in [city] or remote." A late "no" wastes everyone's time.)*
:::

:::warn Don't
- Promise a joining date you can't control.
- Claim you're "serving notice" when you haven't resigned.
- Say yes to relocation or shifts you won't actually accept.
:::

### Do's and don'ts

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Use STAR and say "I" for your actions | Hide behind "we" or give generic answers |
| Use real numbers, framed honestly ("roughly", "measured by…") | Invent stories, numbers or technologies |
| Research the company and mention one specific fact | Recite the company website or invent facts |
| Speak positively about past employers | Criticise your manager, team or client |
| Say "I don't know, but here's how I'd find out" | Bluff when you don't know |
| Keep answers to 1–2 minutes and check in ("Should I go deeper?") | Monologue for five minutes |
| Admit mistakes and show what you learned | Claim you've never failed |
| Respect client confidentiality | Share client names, data or code |
| Ask 2–3 thoughtful questions at the end | Say "No questions" |
| Send a short thank-you note after the interview | Chase the recruiter every day |
| Test camera, mic and internet before a video call | Join late, from a noisy place |

### Day-before-interview checklist

**Definition.** A one-page checklist for the evening before, so you walk in prepared and calm.

**Why it matters.** Preparation reduces anxiety, and most "bad interview" stories come from avoidable things: a forgotten story, a dead laptop, an unclear address.

**Logistics**
- Confirm the time, time zone, interview format and the interviewer's name. Save the meeting link or address.
- Video call: test camera, microphone, internet and screen sharing. Charge the laptop, close extra apps, silence notifications. Keep a phone hotspot as backup.
- In person: plan the route to arrive 15 minutes early. Know where to report and whom to ask for.

**Documents**
- Updated resume (the same version they have), ID proof, and offer letters or payslips only if asked.
- A notepad and pen for notes and for drawing architecture.

**Your stories**
- Say "Tell me about yourself" out loud once with a timer (60–90 seconds).
- Rehearse your project explanation and draw the architecture once from memory.
- Review your 6–8 STAR stories: performance fix, production incident, technical decision, challenge, achievement, failure, conflict, client situation.
- Prepare your strengths (with evidence) and one genuine weakness (with an improvement plan).

**Technical refresh (light, not cramming)**
- Re-read your resume and be ready to explain every technology on it.
- Skim the core topics for the role: C# and OOP, ASP.NET Core and DI, EF Core and SQL, your frontend framework, Azure basics.

**Company and role**
- Re-read the job description. Note two skills to highlight and one specific fact from your research.
- Prepare 2–3 questions for the interviewer.

**Numbers**
- Know your current pay, your expected range, your notice period and your earliest joining date.

**You**
- Lay out clothes, sleep well, eat before the interview, and keep water nearby.
- Stop preparing at a fixed time. A rested mind beats one more hour of revision.

## Quick-fire Q&A

:::q Why should we hire you?
"Because I can contribute from the first month. I already build ASP.NET Core APIs, Angular front ends and Azure deployments, which is the core of this role. I own work end to end, including production support, and I can explain technical issues to business people. I'd also bring [one specific strength from the JD match]."
:::

:::q What do you know about our company?
Give two or three facts you verified yourself (what they do, the practice you're joining, one recent item from their news), then link one of them to your experience. Never guess or make facts up.
:::

:::q Are you interviewing with other companies? Do you have other offers?
Be honest and brief: "Yes, I'm in process with a couple of companies, but this role is my first preference because of [reason]." If you have an offer, say so without using it as a threat; it can speed up their process.
:::

:::q Why have you changed jobs so often? (Or: why have you stayed so long in one company?)
For frequent moves, give a brief, positive reason for each and show the pattern of growth; then state that you're looking for a long-term role. For a long stay, show growth inside it: new responsibilities, modules and technologies, so it reads as depth, not stagnation.
:::

:::q Can you explain the gap in your resume?
State the reason briefly and honestly (health, family, layoff, study), then move to what you did to stay current: courses, certifications, personal projects. Don't over-explain or apologise.
:::

:::q What motivates you?
"Solving real problems for users and seeing the result in production, such as a page that's now fast or a manual process that disappeared. I'm also motivated by learning; I like teams where I keep getting harder problems."
:::

:::q How do you keep your skills up to date?
"I follow the .NET and Azure release notes and blogs, try new features in small side projects, and do structured learning on Microsoft Learn. When something useful comes out, I share it with my team in a short tech talk or an internal write-up."
:::

:::q Are you comfortable working in shifts, with overseas clients or on call?
Answer honestly and specifically: "Yes, I currently overlap with UK hours until about 8 p.m. and I'm on a weekly support rotation." If you have limits, state them clearly now rather than after the offer.
:::

:::q How do you prioritise when you have several urgent tasks?
"I check impact and deadline for each, confirm priorities with my lead or product owner when they compete, and tell people early what will move. Production issues affecting users come first; everything else is ordered by business impact."
:::

:::q What would your manager say about you?
Quote real feedback if you can: "In my last appraisal my manager said I'm reliable and the person he trusts with production issues. He also said I should present more in client meetings, which I've started doing in sprint demos."
:::

:::q Are you open to working on technologies other than .NET?
"Yes. .NET is my strength, but I've worked with Angular, React, SQL and Azure, and I learn new tools quickly. I'd like .NET to stay my core, but I'm happy to pick up whatever the project needs."
:::

:::q Do you have any questions for us?
Always yes. Ask two or three prepared questions about the first three months, what success looks like, how the team works, and the next steps. Never say "No, everything is clear."
:::
